On Wednesday, Oct. 14, 2026, researchers at the University of Minnesota released a decision-making guide to help local governments navigate potential data center proposals from large technology companies like Google and Meta, as reported by MPR News.

The guide capped a yearlong joint project between the Humphrey School of Public Affairs, the University of Minnesota Law School, University of Minnesota Extension, and the nonprofit Freshwater Society. Initial research funding for the southeast Minnesota phase came from the university's Institute on the Environment. Dr. Elise Harrington, an assistant professor at the Humphrey School, led the project alongside law professor James Coleman.

Researchers worked directly with municipal leaders from three southeastern Minnesota cities, Byron, Stewartville, and Lewiston, to base the work on everyday municipal needs. As MPR News reported, Byron City Administrator Al Roder approached Extension alongside his peers to gather objective data after watching disputes arise in other communities.

Roder said municipal leaders wanted to answer questions with facts rather than emotional arguments. He explained that establishing rules before a developer knocks on the door makes it easier to decide what conditions a community finds acceptable, rather than trying to decide in the heat of battle.

The initiative followed debates in nearby Pine Island, about 20 minutes from Byron, over Google's multibillion-dollar Project Skyway proposal. According to MPR News, that proposal caused local disagreements over non-disclosure agreements, heavy power demands, and water usage, leading several political newcomers to run for municipal office.

The resulting toolkit functions as a customizable guide for cities reviewing hyperscale data centers, which are massive computer facilities that support internet services. U of M Extension noted that the toolkit outlines state and regional regulations, suggests model language for local zoning and code updates, and lists specific questions officials can ask tech developers regarding intense electricity demands and municipal water cooling.

Harrington explained that the guide does not tell a town whether to approve or reject a project. Instead, it aims to help local governments think through questions beforehand so they can negotiate from a proactive position.

Natural resources play a direct role in those decisions. Andi Sutton, executive director of Extension's Southeast Regional Sustainable Development Partnership, noted that southeast Minnesota has porous karst rock formations that connect groundwater across municipal boundaries. Sutton warned that because aquifers are sensitive, groundwater use in one community quickly affects neighboring towns, making coordination between local governments essential.

More than 60 planners, administrators, and elected officials gathered in Rochester at the Wood Lake Meeting Center on Oct. 9 to review draft recommendations, MPR News reported. Greg Krushke, the community development manager for Owatonna, attended the meeting to apply the findings as his city updates its zoning and subdivision codes from the 1980s.

Following the public release, researchers and the Freshwater Society scheduled an informational webinar for Wednesday evening from 6:00 p.m. to 7:00 p.m. to help city leaders across Minnesota implement the toolkit.
