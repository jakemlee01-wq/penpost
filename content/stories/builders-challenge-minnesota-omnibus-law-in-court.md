On Monday, October 19, 2026, Rochester Area Builders and the Central Minnesota Builders Association joined a legal challenge against Minnesota's 2024 omnibus law. The lawsuit is led by attorneys with the Upper Midwest Law Center, who announced the action during a Monday morning press conference, as reported by KROC-AM News.

The plaintiffs are asking a state judge to declare the entire 1,400-page measure unconstitutional and void rather than striking only specific clauses. The filing marks the fifth separate legal challenge brought against the package. The legislation was assembled and passed by the DFL trifecta during the final hours of the 2024 legislative session amid Republican filibustering and floor protests. Opponents have nicknamed the law "omnibus prime," "taximus maximus," and the "Jumbo Omnibus."

At the core of the lawsuit is Article IV, Section 17 of the Minnesota Constitution, known as the single-subject rule. The clause mandates that every bill embrace only one subject, and that the subject be clearly expressed in its title. The complaint argues the omnibus measure improperly combined dozens of unrelated policy areas, including pay rates for Uber and Lyft drivers, paid family leave, gun regulations, combative sports, tax changes, and veterinary licensing.

For the two builder associations, the legal challenge specifically targets three major construction regulations enacted in the bill. The first is a set of stricter residential energy code requirements, including a rule that requires the state code to reduce net residential energy consumption by 70% by 2038. The second is a new 14-part statutory test that severely restricts the classification of construction workers as independent contractors. The third is a newly enacted mandatory contractor registration requirement.

The lawsuit asserts that these mandates will directly affect home construction. According to the complaint, reported by Minnesota News Network, the new building requirements will increase the average cost of building a typical new two-story house in Minnesota by $44,000.

The legal strategy follows an earlier ruling on the same legislative package. In a previous challenge brought by the Minnesota Gun Owners Caucus, Ramsey County District Court Judge Leonardo Castro struck down an unrelated binary-trigger ban in the omnibus bill on single-subject grounds, a decision later affirmed by the Minnesota Court of Appeals. In that ruling, Judge Castro noted that combining disparate provisions in a single bill opened the legislation to future legal strikes.
