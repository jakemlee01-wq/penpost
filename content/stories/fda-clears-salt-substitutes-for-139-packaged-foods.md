The U.S. Food and Drug Administration issued a final rule on Tuesday, Oct. 13, 2026, that allows food manufacturers to use salt substitutes across 139 standardized food categories. As reported by the Center for Science in the Public Interest and the National Law Review, the decision updates federal standards of identity, which are the official regulations that define what specific foods must contain to carry their names.

According to reporting from the National Law Review, Keller and Heckman LLP, and Tortilla News, the regulation updates 80 food standards directly and another 59 through cross-references. These categories previously required or permitted conventional table salt, known chemically as sodium chloride, without allowing alternatives.

The change affects a wide selection of everyday grocery items. Reports from the Center for Science in the Public Interest and FoodNavigator-USA note that the updated categories include breads, dinner rolls, cheeses, sour cream, canned fruits and vegetables, canned tuna and seafood, pasta, salad dressings, chocolate, and condiments.

Under the rule, published under Docket No. FDA-2022-N-2226 following public display on Oct. 9, the agency added Section 130.30 to Title 21 of the Code of Federal Regulations. Law firm Keller and Heckman LLP reported that this section defines a salt substitute as a safe and suitable ingredient, or combination of ingredients, used to replace some or all added salt to lower sodium while still performing salt's functional role in food.

The agency is not limiting manufacturers to a single option. As reported by the Center for Science in the Public Interest and Just Food, companies can use alternatives such as potassium salt, herbs, and spices, provided they are approved food additives or generally recognized as safe. Just Food reported that manufacturers may only swap salt to the extent that the replacement keeps the product's basic nature, essential characteristics, safety, and nutritional quality intact.

Tortilla News and the Center for Science in the Public Interest reported that the policy requires no front-of-package warning symbols. Instead, food companies must list the substitutes by their common names, such as potassium salt, in the ingredient list and update their Nutrition Facts panels.

Federal dietary data cited by the Center for Science in the Public Interest and FoodNavigator-USA shows American adults consume an average of roughly 3,100 milligrams of sodium each day. That is about 35 percent higher than the federal recommended daily limit of 2,300 milligrams, raising risks for cardiovascular disease, high blood pressure, and stroke, which affect nearly half of American adults. The FDA stated that permitting salt substitutes supports broader sodium reduction efforts and aligns with the Make America Healthy Again initiative.

The final rule completes an effort that began when the Center for Science in the Public Interest petitioned the FDA in 2018. Eva Greenthal of the group noted that public health advocates had formally recommended the update since that year. The agency later allowed the term "potassium salt" in 2020 guidance and issued its proposed rule in April 2023.

Michael Dykes, president and chief executive of the International Dairy Foods Association, told Perishable News that the group welcomes the rule because it provides dairy manufacturers with flexibility to reduce sodium while maintaining quality and safety. Meanwhile, the Center for Science in the Public Interest stated that the responsibility now falls on the food industry to change product recipes, while calling on the FDA to finalize mandatory front-of-package sodium warning labels.

As reported by Just Food and Food Safety Magazine, the regulation takes effect on Dec. 14, 2026. Affected parties have until Nov. 12, 2026, to file formal objections regarding specific dairy standards, and the FDA plans to release a report later this year reviewing industry progress under voluntary reduction targets.
