The U.S. Food and Drug Administration launched the Rare Disease Framework Initiative on Oct. 14, 2026, setting out to build a new regulatory structure for reviewing treatments for rare conditions. The plan, outlined in an agency white paper titled "A New Framework for Rare Disease Review," comes as drug developers in clinical trials fail more than 90% of the time, according to reporting by BioPharma Dive. That outlet noted drug sponsors face steep hurdles in rare diseases because little natural-history data exists to show how those conditions typically progress.

The initiative is spearheaded by the FDA's Rare Disease Innovation Hub alongside the Office of the Commissioner, the Center for Drug Evaluation and Research, and the Center for Biologics Evaluation and Research, with support from the medical device center, CDRH, as reported by RAPS. Acting FDA Commissioner Kyle Diamantas stated that the agency aims to build a more consistent, predictable, and innovative approach while keeping rigorous standards. Diamantas also noted that patients and families facing rare diseases have few or no approved treatments, leaving them with uncertainty across every part of their lives.

According to the agency white paper and BioPharma Dive, roughly 30 million Americans and 300 million people globally live with rare conditions across 7,000 to 10,000 distinct illnesses, but only about 5% have an FDA-approved drug. At the same time, the FDA's drug and biologics review centers report that about half of their recent novel drug approvals have been for rare diseases. Amy Comstock Rick, director of the Rare Disease Innovation Hub, told Healthcare Dive that families deserve a process addressing the uniqueness of each condition rather than a one-size-fits-all model. Health and Human Services Secretary Robert F. Kennedy, Jr. stated that Americans cannot afford a system that treats every condition alike, adding that under President Trump, the FDA is rethinking development to put patients at the center.

To change the review process, the FDA plans to look across the entire development pipeline, from preclinical trial designs to requirements after a drug hits the market. BioPharma Dive reported that the agency wants to shift away from default demands for conventional randomized trials or standard statistical thresholds, leaning instead toward evidence tailored to small groups of patients. The agency intends to encourage novel trial designs, new statistical tools, flexible biomarker tests, and tailored surrogate endpoints, which are substitute measurements used when direct clinical outcomes take too long to observe. According to RAPS, the initiative also introduces risk-based reviews for chemistry, manufacturing, and controls, which oversee how a medicine is made and tested for quality, along with pre-approval factory inspections.

The National Organization for Rare Disorders issued a statement praising the initiative, saying it directly responds to concerns patient groups brought to the Acting Commissioner in June. Next, the FDA and the Reagan-Udall Foundation for the FDA have scheduled a public hybrid meeting for Oct. 28, 2026, at the agency's White Oak campus in Silver Spring and online via Zoom. The FDA also published a Request for Information across four core focus areas, with Bloomberg News reporting that the agency is targeting February 2027 to deliver its first formal regulatory recommendations.
