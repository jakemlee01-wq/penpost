A federal effort to expand domestic fertilizer manufacturing is directing money into modular production units across the Midwest, including locations planned for Minnesota.

The U.S. Department of Agriculture selected Texas-based TalusAg for approximately $99.8 million in proposed funding toward an overall $270.2 million project, according to reporting from the Grand Rapids Herald-Review and Brownfield Ag News. TalusAg plans to deploy 20 modular ammonia manufacturing units across 11 separate locations spread over seven states: Minnesota, Iowa, Kentucky, Tennessee, Nebraska, Illinois, and South Dakota. Regional news reports noted that while Minnesota is part of the seven-state footprint, the USDA announcement did not disclose the specific towns or parcels chosen for the local units.

According to Brownfield Ag News, TalusAg developed the first commercial green ammonia system built to produce fertilizer on-site and keep localized distribution cost-competitive. The USDA and Credence Research reported that the modular units will use domestic energy, air, and water to produce roughly 130,000 tons of anhydrous ammonia each year for local agricultural markets. Developers are expected to break ground within weeks, with construction projected to finish in 2027 or 2028, according to The Gazette and the Iowa Capital Dispatch.

The award is part of a broader rollout announced on Thursday, Oct. 22, 2026, by Vice President J.D. Vance and Agriculture Secretary Brooke Rollins, with Vance speaking during an appearance in Nebraska. The initiative falls under the USDA's Fertilizer Investment and Expansion for Long-Term Domestic Supply program, known as FIELDS. Credence Research and No-Till Farmer reported that FIELDS was established on July 1, 2026, using $500 million under Commodity Credit Corporation authority, alongside reallocated funds recovered from terminated projects under the prior administration's Fertilizer Production Expansion Program. The initiative is administered through USDA Rural Development's Rural Business-Cooperative Service.

Across the country, the USDA selected eight domestic fertilizer projects representing more than $1.34 billion in total proposed public and private investments, including about $573 million in USDA funding. The agency expects the eight projects to support about 1.08 million tons of annual domestic production capacity.

In justifying the program, the USDA pointed to heavy market concentration, noting that four companies control approximately 77% of North American nitrogen production capacity, as reported by Feed and Grain and No-Till Farmer. The USDA stated that farm security is national security, adding that the country cannot afford to rely on foreign nations for fertilizer needed to grow food. Feed and Grain reported that the USDA estimates about 1.87 million farmers working over 874 million acres will gain access to cheaper domestic supplies.

Agency economic analyses project that the combined facilities will reduce domestic nitrogen fertilizer prices by 4.4% to 4.5% and potash prices by 5% to 5.3%. The USDA also estimates that the new facilities will lift national nitrogen production by 3.7% and raise domestic potash output by nearly 40%.
