The Federal Trade Commission and the U.S. Department of Agriculture have opened a joint public inquiry examining potential anticompetitive practices across agricultural equipment manufacturing, distribution, and repair markets.

The inquiry follows a growing volume of formal complaints submitted to the USDA by agricultural producers, as reported by The Wall Street Journal and Progressive Farmer. Those filings detailed steep maintenance costs, artificial obstacles to purchasing machinery, difficulties securing replacement parts, and extended waits for repair services.

Deputy Secretary Stephen Vaden reported that the agencies published a request for information containing 13 questions for agricultural producers, with an option to submit comments anonymously. According to reporting from AGDAILY and AgWeb, regulators are seeking testimony from farmers, independent repair shops, and current or former employees of equipment manufacturers and dealerships regarding restrictive dealer contracts, penalties, and retaliation. The public docket on Regulations.gov will remain open for submissions until December 7, 2026, at 11:59 p.m. Eastern Time.

According to Michigan Farm Bureau and AGDAILY, the inquiry specifically examines manufacturer policies such as territorial boundary rules that bar dealers from selling or servicing equipment across designated territory lines. Regulators are also reviewing contractual arrangements that require equipment owners to purchase parts and maintenance exclusively from authorized dealerships.

In a post on X reported by Farms.com, Agriculture Secretary Brooke Rollins stated that she regularly hears from producers about restrictions on repairing their own machinery, warning that these limits create expensive delays and lost labor hours during harvest season. FTC Chair Andrew Ferguson noted in remarks cited by the Michigan Farm Bureau that farmers should not face artificial service barriers, inflated equipment pricing, or retaliation for repairing their own machinery or comparison shopping.

Early filings on the public docket illustrate some of the expenses farmers encounter, according to Farms.com. South Dakota producer Richard Coelho submitted a comment stating that a dealership charged him $520 to sharpen a baler knife and $786 to replace a $40 switch that the repair shop had incorrectly wired. Another docket complaint cited a service center that refused to service a 2020 John Deere S770 combine.

Following the inquiry announcement, shares of major agricultural machinery manufacturers declined. Reporting from Transport Topics and Investing.com noted that Deere & Co. stock dropped between 3% and 5.7%, AGCO Corporation fell 5.6% to 8.4%, CNH Industrial dropped 5.3% to 7.3%, and Caterpillar fell up to 6.4%.

The joint inquiry builds on earlier federal enforcement actions. In July 2026, a settlement between the FTC, five states, and Deere & Company mandated that the manufacturer provide independent repair shops and farmers with the same diagnostic tools, software, and manuals provided to franchised dealers. Regulations.gov and Farm Progress reported that the current review also follows a September 28, 2026 settlement prohibiting Corteva Inc. from using exclusionary pesticide loyalty programs for 10 years, as well as an FTC antitrust investigation opened in May 2026 into commercial fertilizer pricing.

In addition to the machinery inquiry, the USDA announced it is expanding joint enforcement with the Department of Justice to investigate anticompetitive licensing and pricing practices in the agricultural seed sector, as reported by Iowa Capital Dispatch. According to Bloomberg Intelligence and Regulations.gov, feedback gathered by the December 7 deadline will guide enforcement actions, potential administrative rulemaking, and policy remedies under Executive Order 14364, which targets rural living and production costs.
