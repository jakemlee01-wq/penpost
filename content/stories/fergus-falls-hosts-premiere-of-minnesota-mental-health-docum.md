Pioneer PBS held the premiere screening of its documentary, "Lost Souls: Reforming Minnesota's Mental Health Institutions," at A Center for the Arts in Fergus Falls. The West Central Tribune reported that the city was chosen because it is home to the landmark former Fergus Falls Regional Treatment Center, one of the largest psychiatric facilities in Greater Minnesota.

The 27-minute film is based on the book "The Crusade for Forgotten Souls: Reforming Minnesota's Mental Institutions, 1946 to 1954" by Susan Bartlett Foote, a retired University of Minnesota public health professor, according to reporting from MPR News and Detroit Lakes Online. Susan Marks directed and produced the film alongside producer Dawn Mikkelson for Pioneer PBS.

The documentary examines the state's historical institutional care, chronicling overcrowding, severe underfunding, patient neglect, and abuse at regional state hospitals. The film highlights how grassroots family advocacy, caregivers, and reform leaders mobilized to transform the system, tracing the shift away from large state institutions toward community-based care and outpatient support.

MPR News reported that local resident Bill Adams described Fergus Falls as central to the project because book subject Anders Schey was committed to the Fergus Falls State Hospital 100 years ago. Schey's daughter, Engla Schey, took a job working inside other Minnesota state hospitals, witnessed abuses firsthand, and became a leading crusader pushing for fair patient treatment across the state.

According to Otter Tail County Historical Society records and PBS LearningMedia, Minnesota opened its first state asylum in St. Peter in 1866. Facilities followed in Rochester and Fergus Falls, with the Fergus Falls campus opening in 1890 under the Kirkbride Plan, an architectural design intended to serve therapeutic purposes.

Detroit Lakes Online reported that Minnesota state hospitals once housed more than 10,000 patients. That population dropped to roughly 3,000 by 1970 following the federal Community Mental Health Act, a law designed to move care into outpatient settings.

That rapid shift created new hardships. Detroit Lakes Online reported that Foote recounted an interview with former Fergus Falls staff members who cried over unprepared discharges. Foote quoted a worker who said staff knew the patients well and knew they were being sent to places that would not work for them.

Director Susan Marks stated that families experienced significant pain and shame when a loved one was sent away, and she described former asylum sites as places of remembrance that need to be preserved and kept in peace.

The free Fergus Falls event concluded with an audience question-and-answer session featuring Foote, Marks, Mikkelson, and retired Otter Tail County Historical Society Director Chris Schuelke.

Pioneer PBS scheduled television broadcasts to follow the regional preview tour, airing the documentary concurrently on October 15 at 7:00 p.m., with encore airings on Sunday, October 18 at 7:00 p.m. and Monday, October 19 at 1:30 p.m. The station also made the film available for on-demand streaming on pioneer.org, PBS.org, and the PBS YouTube channel.
