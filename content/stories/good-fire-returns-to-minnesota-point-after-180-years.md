On a sandy strip of land between Lake Superior and Superior Bay, an old-growth forest waited roughly 180 years for a spark it actually needed. On Thursday, October 8, 2026, fire crews carried out a culturally guided prescribed burn across 17 acres of Minnesota Point, according to reporting by MPR News and ICT. The burn reintroduced intentional fire stewardship to the peninsula for the first time in nearly two centuries.

The project carries the Ojibwe name Zhaagawaamikong-neyaashi Azhenamaw Manidoo-ishkode: Returning the Spirit of Good Fire to Minnesota Point. MPR News and ICT reported that Fond du Lac Band elder Ricky DeFoe opened the morning gathering with a traditional pipe and fire ceremony. He ignited birch and cedar bark collected directly from the point. Addressing roughly two dozen tribal and state fire specialists, DeFoe stated that the objective is to connect and reconnect to Mother Earth.

Vern Northrup, a Fond du Lac Band elder, burn boss, and 25-year wildland firefighting veteran, lit the ceremonial flame and carried its embers into the woods. Northrup explained that fire is what has been missing from environmental management, stating that its absence is why forests become overgrown and catastrophic fires break out.

The low-intensity burn treated a stand of red and white pines where natural cycles had stalled. Research cited by the University of Minnesota Duluth and the 1854 Treaty Authority shows that when Anishinaabe displacement ended traditional burns nearly two centuries ago, fallen needles and brush choked the forest floor. Over the last 120 years, only a single red pine had naturally regenerated in the stand's interior canopy.

The low creeping fire acts like a reset button for that crowded floor. As the Duluth News Tribune reported, the fire aimed to trigger red pine seed germination, cycle soil nutrients, and revive patches of wild blueberries and gooseberries. Clearing out the accumulated debris also reduces fuel for future severe wildfires. The project grew out of tree-ring and Indigenous ecological research led by Dr. Evan Larson of the University of Wisconsin-Platteville and Red Cliff tribal member Melonee Montano.

Executing the burn required a multi-agency partnership. The Fond du Lac Band worked alongside the Red Cliff Band, the City of Duluth, the Minnesota Department of Natural Resources Scientific and Natural Areas program, and the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service. Bazile Panek, an enrolled Red Cliff member and chief executive of consulting firm Good Sky Guidance, served as project lead under a three-year federal agreement, with nonprofit Minnesota Point 50 as fiscal sponsor.

City of Duluth Natural Resources Coordinator Kate Kubiak highlighted that many local forests have been starved of disturbance for decades, adding that she is eager to see the land respond, according to the 1854 Treaty Authority. The burn follows a similar 2025 burn across the harbor on Wisconsin Point. Partners plan to monitor vegetative recovery on the 17 acres and expand the cultural burn to an adjacent 24 acres next year.
