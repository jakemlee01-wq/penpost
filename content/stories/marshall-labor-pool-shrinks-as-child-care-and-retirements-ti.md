Over the past 15 years, Marshall's labor force fell by 11.1 percent compared to 2010 levels, even as Minnesota's statewide labor force grew by 7.8 percent over the same period, MPR News reported. In August, Marshall registered an unemployment rate of 4.7 percent, which sat slightly below the statewide average.

While Marshall's overall population has grown, the pool of available workers is contracting. Reporting by MPR News points to two main forces behind the shift: shortages of child care and an aging population.

U.S. Census data shows that residents aged 65 and older made up 11.4 percent of Marshall's population in 2010, rising to 17.1 percent in the latest estimates. Luke Greiner, a regional analyst with the Minnesota Department of Employment and Economic Development, told MPR News that the change reflects baby boomers phasing into retirement. Greiner noted that broader projections point toward long-term population stability and slow job growth rather than regional decline.

Marshall Mayor Bob Byrnes noted that the city's population nearly doubles during the day as commuters arrive to work for major regional employers, including Turkey Valley Farms, Avera Medical Group, and Southwest Minnesota State University. Mayor Byrnes stated that the community has residents who would work if they had available and affordable child care, noting that a much larger need remains despite existing centers.

A 2025 state-funded report found that the Marshall area faces a deficit of more than 400 child care slots, MPR News reported. Greiner stated that home-based family providers are declining steadily because of business costs and stringent regulations. Kim Swalboski reported that retirements among veteran providers are compounding the gap, with few younger operators entering the field to replace them.

Local provider Michele Gregoire stated that her facility is completely full and cannot expand due to staffing shortages, leaving her waiting list with no openings until 2028. Providers told MPR News that infant care faces the tightest crunch because state rules require a ratio of one adult for every four infants. Kari Dahl Condezo noted that revenue in the infant room is not enough to support the teacher.

International newcomers are helping drive Marshall's overall population growth, according to DEED and MPR News, but many encounter language barriers and licensing hurdles while seeking care. At Southwest Minnesota State University, Kezya Silva attended an English class alongside her one-year-old son, Leonardo, because daycare was unavailable. Silva stated that she is not working because of her son. Stacy Abraham told MPR News that many students possess strong skills but cannot fully enter the local economy without baseline community support like accessible care. Greiner added that multiple overlapping factors explain the disconnect, with no single cause behind it.
