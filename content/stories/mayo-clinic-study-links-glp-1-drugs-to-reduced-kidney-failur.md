Adults living with bipolar disorder and chronic kidney disease often balance complex medication schedules to protect their mental health and their vital organs. A study announced by Mayo Clinic researchers on Monday, Oct. 19, 2026, found that GLP-1 receptor agonists are associated with a lower risk of dialysis or kidney transplantation in this group of patients.

The findings were published in the Journal of Affective Disorders and led by senior author Dr. Balwinder Singh, a psychiatrist at Mayo Clinic. The Mayo Clinic News Network reported that chronic kidney disease is disproportionately common among people with bipolar disorder, driven by higher rates of hypertension, diabetes, and obesity, as well as the long-term kidney monitoring required for psychiatric treatments.

As reported by News-Medical, researchers evaluated de-identified records from TriNetX, an international research database encompassing roughly 131 million adults across 117 health systems. The investigation focused on adults diagnosed with bipolar disorder and mild-to-moderate chronic kidney disease, spanning stages 1 through 3, who had newly started a GLP-1 medication.

Investigators followed patients for up to five years, using statistical balancing across demographics and baseline health markers. News-Medical reported that the study compared GLP-1 users against matched groups taking DPP-4 inhibitors or renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system inhibitors, known as RAAS inhibitors.

According to figures published in the Journal of Affective Disorders, patients on GLP-1 drugs showed better kidney outcomes than both comparison groups. When compared to DPP-4 inhibitors, GLP-1 use showed an adjusted hazard ratio of 0.56 for kidney replacement therapy, which refers to dialysis or a kidney transplant, and an adjusted hazard ratio of 0.74 for healthcare utilization. The five-year rate of remaining free from kidney replacement therapy was 87.93% for GLP-1 users compared to 84.63% for DPP-4 users.

Against RAAS inhibitors, the Journal of Affective Disorders reported that GLP-1 users had an adjusted hazard ratio of 0.66 for kidney replacement therapy. In that group, the five-year survival rate without kidney replacement therapy reached 94.68% for GLP-1 patients, compared to 89.77% for those taking RAAS inhibitors.

News-Medical reported that the kidney-protective association remained consistent across sex, race, ethnicity, obesity status, and other psychiatric prescriptions. Mayo Clinic noted that the lower risk also held true for patients taking lithium, a foundational bipolar medication that carries chronic kidney disease risks and requires routine kidney monitoring.

Dr. Singh stated that while people with bipolar disorder carry a high burden of kidney disease due to metabolic risks, few strategies have been studied specifically to protect their kidney health. He noted that protecting kidney function in this population often requires care across multiple medical specialties, adding that confirmed findings could help teams protect organs while continuing psychiatric treatment.

Because the study analyzed observational records, Mayo Clinic News Network and News-Medical noted that it cannot prove cause and effect, nor could it track daily pill adherence, exact dosing variations, or therapy changes over time. Mayo Clinic authors stressed that randomized clinical trials and prospective studies are required before updating clinical guidelines across psychiatry, nephrology, and primary care. News-Medical noted that the work connects to Mayo Clinic's Genesis initiative, which focuses on preserving vital organ function.
