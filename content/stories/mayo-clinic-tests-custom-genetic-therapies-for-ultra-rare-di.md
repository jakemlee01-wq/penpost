On laboratory screens in Rochester, clusters of living human brain cells glow under specialized microscopes. Those cultured neurons grew from a skin sample donated by Summer Nagele, a 21-year-old athlete from Illinois who lives with an ultra-rare genetic disease.

Mayo Clinic announced details of the individualized research framework behind that work, run through the Center for Individualized Medicine's N-of-1 Therapeutics Program. The initiative is built to develop tailored genetic therapies for conditions that affect only a tiny group of patients worldwide, or even a single person.

The laboratory effort is directed by Dr. Margot Cousin, a clinical and translational scientist and assistant professor of medical genetics at Mayo Clinic. Working alongside first-year virology and gene therapy graduate student Kaitlyn Dirks and technologist Han Lee, the team is focusing on KCNK9 imprinting syndrome.

The condition is a severe neurodevelopmental disorder diagnosed in fewer than 100 people across the world. It causes central hypotonia, a form of severe low muscle tone that impairs speech, movement, swallowing, and lung capacity. The disease also frequently leads to debilitating spinal curvature. Nagele, an eight-sport Special Olympics competitor, had spinal fusion surgery for scoliosis when she was 13.

Using Nagele's cellular models, the team is screening and testing custom antisense oligonucleotides, known as ASOs. These are synthetic molecules formulated to modify genetic instructions inside human cells.

Dr. Cousin explained that for decades, medicine could usually only offer patients a name for their condition. A diagnosis remains an extraordinary milestone, she said, but the goal now is turning that answer into a pipeline toward treatment for illnesses previously considered untreatable.

The foundation for this work began earlier. In 2022, Dr. Cousin's team discovered 15 previously unidentified mutations in the maternal KCNK9 gene, as reported by the Mayo Clinic News Network and Genome Medicine. That discovery helped 21 families around the world secure an exact diagnosis.

The program collaborates directly with the patient community, including the KCNK9 Foundation. The nonprofit was started by Melissa and Sean Kelley, whose 12-year-old son, Preston, lives with a severe form of the disease. Dr. Cousin recently joined families at the third Global KCNK9 Imprinting Syndrome Symposium in Dallas, Texas.

When Nagele gave her skin biopsy, she recalled naming other affected children during the procedure to remind herself of all the kids she was helping. Her father, Michael Nagele, said Dr. Cousin has become family to them.

Mayo Clinic plans to use this framework as a repeatable template for other academic medical centers developing gene and molecular treatments for single-patient conditions. The N-of-1 pipeline has already delivered experimental therapies to a Mayo Clinic patient with autosomal dominant leukodystrophy in 2025, and researchers reported an experimental ASO targeting a mutation in ALS in October 2026, according to the Mayo Clinic News Network and Med.

To prepare for future human trials, Dr. Cousin's team has launched a comprehensive natural history study. The project will track KCNK9 patients over time to identify the biological markers and clinical endpoints needed for upcoming clinical studies.
