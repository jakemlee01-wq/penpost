On Monday, Oct. 26, 2026, four medical societies published a joint three-part clinical compendium in the journal Circulation, establishing a shared framework to evaluate and treat women's heart health from childhood through late adulthood, according to reports from Northwestern University and Northwestern Now. The document brings together the American Heart Association, the American Academy of Pediatrics, the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists, and the Society for Maternal-Fetal Medicine.

Dr. Sadiya S. Khan served as chair of the collaborative writing group, alongside vice chair Dr. Amanda Marma Perak of Northwestern, and key authors Dr. Natalie Cameron and former American Heart Association volunteer president Dr. Stacey E. Rosen. Dr. Khan stated that prior care for women had been siloed across separate medical specialties, resulting in fragmented treatment, comparing the effort to creating a new sport with a single playbook. Dr. Perak stated that cardiovascular health begins well before adulthood, pointing to pediatric checkups as an early opportunity for evaluation.

Two of the manuscripts outline a clinical model organized around four life stages: adolescence, reproductive years, perimenopause, and postmenopause, as reported by Professional Heart Daily. Under this framework, reproductive milestones and issues such as early menstruation patterns, preeclampsia, gestational diabetes, and hormone therapy are identified as direct cardiovascular risk enhancers. Circulation noted that pregnancy-related complications specifically require proactive postpartum cardiology follow-up.

Cardiovascular disease remains the leading cause of death among American women, claiming the lives of approximately one in every three women each year, according to reporting by Northwestern Now and TIME. Northwestern reported that progress in lowering female heart disease mortality rates has largely stalled over the past two decades. Additionally, an American Heart Association survey cited in the compendium found that fewer than half of women know cardiovascular disease is their leading health threat, as reported by TIME. Dr. Rosen stated that heart disease does not become relevant only as women age, calling the framework a paradigm shift for women's cardiovascular and brain health.

The third publication outlines plans for a national network of Comprehensive Centers of Excellence for Women's Cardiovascular Care focused on clinical care, training, research, and community engagement, according to the American Heart Association and Professional Heart Daily. The groups are releasing clinical toolkits, educational case series, and podcasts to help pediatricians, obstetricians, primary care doctors, and cardiologists adopt shared screening protocols and establish local referral networks.
