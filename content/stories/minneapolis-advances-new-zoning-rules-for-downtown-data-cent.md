Downtown Minneapolis office floors that have sat quiet could soon be filled with rows of servers rather than desks. Under a new proposal, tech companies will not be allowed to put up new standalone data buildings, steering them instead into the city's empty commercial properties.

On Tuesday evening, October 13, 2026, the Minneapolis City Planning Commission met at the Public Service Center on South 4th Street and voted to approve specific zoning amendments for data centers, as reported by KARE 11 and MinneapoliMedia. The action came just weeks ahead of a November 21 deadline, when a six-month moratorium on large-scale data center projects passed by the City Council in May 2026 is set to expire.

Jason Wittenberg, the city's code development manager, informed commissioners that roughly 8 million square feet of downtown commercial space is vacant. As reported by KARE 11, that makes up about 30 percent of the downtown commercial inventory analyzed in the city's study. Wittenberg stated that communities across the country have found their zoning codes are not equipped to properly deal with modern data centers, and Minneapolis is no exception.

Under previous city code, data facilities had no dedicated category, according to reporting by KARE 11 and MinneapoliMedia. They were grouped under "communication exchanges," which allowed them by conditional use permit across 10 of the city's 15 primary zoning districts with no technology-specific rules. Under the new Title 20 amendments, data centers will be classified as their own distinct use. They will only be permitted through a conditional use permit in two downtown commercial districts, DT1 and DT2, which together make up less than 4 percent of the city's land area.

The measure prohibits constructing brand-new standalone buildings solely for data storage. Facilities must be retrofitted into commercial buildings already standing when the ordinance takes effect, with only minor additions allowed to enclose mechanical equipment.

Planning Commissioner Jason Garcia introduced an amendment cutting the maximum permitted white space from the staff-recommended ceiling of 175,000 square feet down to 50,000 square feet, a reduction of approximately 71 percent. Commissioner Angela Conley introduced an amendment requiring an overlay zoning classification instead of primary zoning, ensuring an additional layer of municipal review.

The rules also establish operational boundaries to protect surrounding neighborhoods. Server rooms and mechanical systems are barred from the first and second floors. They must be located at least 20 feet inward from exterior walls and maintain a buffer of at least 100 feet from the exterior wall of any residential home or rooming house. Developers will also have to submit written proof of coordination with electric utilities, file formal sound-attenuation plans, and disclose projected consumption figures for electricity, water, and backup generators.

KARE 11 reported that approximately 40 residents spoke during the public hearing, with the majority opposing data center expansion in Minneapolis due to concerns over electric grid strain, water depletion, and corporate land privatization. One speaker called on the city to extend the moratorium, reject primary zoning, and cap spaces at 25,000 square feet. Another urged leaders to focus on infrastructure that supports the public rather than privatizing land that could promote the common welfare.

Applicants will need to prove compliance with the zoning rules, and commission decisions can be appealed directly to the City Council. The proposal heads to the City Council's Business, Housing and Zoning Committee for review on November 6, 2026, and is scheduled for a vote by the full City Council on November 12, according to KARE 11.
