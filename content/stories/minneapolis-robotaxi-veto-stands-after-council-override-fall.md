A driverless car still needs a human behind the wheel, at least according to a city council plan that just met a firm mayoral stop sign.

On the morning of Thursday, Oct. 15, 2026, the Minneapolis City Council voted 7-5 to override Mayor Jacob Frey's veto of the "Workers Drive Minneapolis" ordinance. As CBS Minnesota, KARE 11, and the Minnesota Reformer reported, the tally fell two votes short of the nine-vote supermajority needed to enact the rules over the mayor's objection.

The council originally approved the measure on Oct. 1, 2026, in a narrow 7-6 vote before Frey vetoed it the very next day. FOX 9 reported that the initial vote was backed by council members Elliott Payne, Robin Wonsley, Jamal Osman, Soren Stevenson, Jason Chavez, Aisha Chughtai, and Aurin Chowdhury.

The proposed rules would have established a municipal licensing system for commercial autonomous vehicles, often called robotaxis. Under the ordinance, any operator would have been required to place a licensed human safety monitor in the driver's seat with immediate access to braking, steering, and acceleration controls. Operators would also have been required to pay those monitors minimum wage rates, according to FOX 9 and the Minnesota Reformer.

Frey opposed the mandate, arguing that requiring a driver in a driverless car never made sense. Reporting by KARE 11 and the Minnesota Reformer quoted Frey calling the measure a back-door ban that runs counter to the city's traditions, while stating that Minneapolis is open for business, open to innovative technology, and open to varied transportation options. He also pointed out that autonomous transit can improve mobility for people with disabilities. During council hearings, representatives from the National Federation of the Blind of Minnesota testified against the ordinance, emphasizing that autonomous vehicles offer vital, accessible transportation, Southwest Voices reported.

Lead author Robin Wonsley countered that the rules were essential to safeguard about 10,000 rideshare drivers across the Twin Cities, many from immigrant communities. Citing research from think tank North Star Policy Action, Wonsley noted that Minnesota rideshare drivers earn $209 million annually and generate $561 million in broader economic activity. According to KARE 11, Wonsley warned that automated vehicles without regulation would disrupt traffic, create safety risks, hurt climate goals, and displace thousands of working families.

Waymo, which began mapping and testing vehicles in Minneapolis in November 2025 without launching commercial rides, openly opposed the plan. Adam Lane, Waymo's state and local public policy manager, called the ordinance a de facto ban. Lane told KARE 11 and the Minnesota Reformer that none of the 15 major U.S. cities where Waymo runs over 500,000 fully autonomous trips each week require an onboard safety monitor.

Following Thursday's vote, Wonsley, Osman, Chavez, and Chowdhury released a joint statement arguing that the sustained veto handed a win to Google, Elon Musk, and Jeff Bezos while leaving working families facing lost income, CBS News reported. Speaking to drivers, Wonsley said council allies would continue fighting so big tech does not take jobs from immigrant workers.

The issue is far from settled. The four council members immediately gave formal notice that they are restarting the legislative process to introduce a revised autonomous vehicle framework in Minneapolis. Meanwhile, statewide debates are anticipated when the Minnesota Legislature convenes in January, according to MPR News. In neighboring St. Paul, where robotaxis have not yet deployed, the City Council voted on Oct. 14, 2026, to direct city staff to proactively study autonomous vehicles and recommend worker and public safety rules.
