Early Monday morning on the shore of Bde Maka Ska, community members gathered at Thomas Beach to welcome the morning sun. Ceremony leader Chaske Henry began the gathering at 7:15 a.m., as attendees offered tobacco, prayed, and held smudging facing the 7:25 a.m. sunrise, MPR News and the Bois Forte Band reported.

Minnesota Lt. Gov. Peggy Flanagan attended the sunrise gathering and read the state's official proclamation. Flanagan highlighted that the name Minnesota comes from the Dakota phrase Mni Sóta Maḳoce, which translates to "Land Where the Waters Reflect the Clouds." Minneapolis City Council Member Jason Chavez attended and highlighted the council's formal resolution. Organizers have held the sunrise ceremony at the lake since 2014, when Minneapolis first voted to recognize Indigenous Peoples Day. Minnesota established it as an official state holiday in 2023, replacing Columbus Day. Later in the day, the Culture Language Arts Network hosted a community powwow from 4:00 to 7:00 p.m. at the Minneapolis American Indian Center.

In St. Louis Park, residents gathered from 10:00 a.m. to 11:30 a.m. at Westwood Hills Nature Center for the fourth annual Ȟaȟá Wakpádaŋ Water Blessing, according to reporting from MinneapoliMedia News and MPR News. Dakota educator Darlene St. Clair gave the opening remarks, and Amara Moore and Ronelle Siert led the blessing for Bassett Creek using Sisseton Dakota and Anishinaabe traditions. The St. Louis Park City Council had approved a proclamation on Oct. 5 recognizing the day and honoring Minnesota's 11 federally recognized tribes, comprising seven Anishinaabe and four Dakota nations.

In Saint Paul, the Tiwahe Foundation reported that Battle Creek Regional Park hosted a midday gathering. It included a water blessing led by Kalpulli Yaocenoxtli, youth performances by the Ikidowin Youth Theater Ensemble, artisan vendors, and a Native youth college fair. On the radio, MPR News host Angela Davis dedicated special broadcast coverage to examining sovereignty, arts, challenges, and future initiatives across Minnesota's 11 tribal nations.

Up in Duluth, the holiday followed a historic return of fire to Minnesota Point. MPR News reported that the Fond du Lac Band of Lake Superior Chippewa, the City of Duluth, the Minnesota Department of Natural Resources, and the U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service carried out a 17-acre low-intensity prescribed burn. Titled Zhaagawaamikong-neyaashi Azhenamaw Manidoo-ishkode, the event marked the first cultural fire stewardship on the peninsula in roughly 180 years. Fond du Lac elder Ricky DeFoe led the opening ceremony, and elder Vern Northrup lit the flame. DeFoe stated that the burn helps people connect and reconnect to Mother Earth, while Northrup explained that intentional burns manage overgrown brush to prevent catastrophic fires.
