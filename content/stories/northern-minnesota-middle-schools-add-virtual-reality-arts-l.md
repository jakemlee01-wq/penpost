Middle school arts students in northern Minnesota are using virtual reality headsets to study muralists, weavers, and musicians. The Perpich Center for Arts Education has partnered with Act One, an Arizona arts education nonprofit, to deploy a virtual reality arts curriculum across rural districts, as reported by the Grand Rapids Herald-Review and XRHQ.

Perpich's Professional Development and Resource Programs team is directing the project toward sixth- through eighth-grade arts classrooms in northwest and northeast Minnesota. Act One first launched its VR Arts Immersion program in Arizona in 2021, reaching more than 50,000 students before beginning this expansion into Minnesota, according to Act One.

Before equipment arrived in the state, Perpich staff traveled to Phoenix for operational training with Act One personnel and virtual reality specialists, XRHQ reported. The sessions covered equipment management, headset operations, and prompts for leading classroom discussions.

On Monday, Oct. 12, 2026, Toni Beckler, a training and development specialist at Perpich, visited Greenway High School in Coleraine to guide students through the equipment and lessons, according to the Grand Rapids Herald-Review. On Oct. 13, 2026, Beckler brought the program to Northeast Range School in the St. Louis County School District, as reported by Hometown Focus and XRHQ.

The curriculum includes two virtual modules, "Freedom in Expression" and "Weaving Our Story." The first module introduces students to Chicago muralist Sentrock along with public art and music careers. The second features the Duncan family, Navajo textile weaver Tyrell Tapaha, and Las Azaleas, an all-female mariachi group from Tucson.

At Greenway High School, visual arts instructor Shari Nelson reported positive student engagement, noting that students quickly entered discussions about cultural storytelling and technique, the Grand Rapids Herald-Review reported. A Greenway student said the program was interesting because it provides more to look at and helps students learn.

At Northeast Range, art instructor Susan Melgeorge told Hometown Focus that it is important for rural communities to learn about other customs, artists, and regions, and to see that everyone is connected. A student at the school remarked that the music made them feel part of the performance.

Beckler stated that arts education can open doors to new perspectives and possibilities, allowing greater Minnesota classrooms to connect with working artists and traditions they might otherwise miss. According to Act One, participating teachers will continue developing customized lesson plans that integrate the virtual content into standard coursework, while Act One and Perpich conduct joint surveys to evaluate classroom outcomes.
