Three bipartisan bills became federal law on Thursday, October 15, 2026, after the president signed measures covering tribal water systems, illegal fishing enforcement, and retirement stock valuations. The White House announced the signings of S. 240, S. 283, and S. 2403.

The first measure, S. 240, is titled the Crow Tribe Water Rights Settlement Amendments Act of 2025. It updates the Crow Tribe Water Rights Settlement Act of 2010 to reshape water infrastructure on the Crow Indian Reservation in Montana. The law changes the municipal, rural, and industrial water program from a single centralized project into a flexible, localized network. Under this framework, the Crow Tribe can build smaller, targeted drinking water and wastewater systems across the reservation.

The bill also extends the tribe's exclusive rights to develop a clean hydropower project at the Yellowtail Afterbay Dam by five years, keeping those rights in place through 2030. S. 240 was introduced in the Senate by Montana Republican Senators Steve Daines and Tim Sheehy, while Montana Representative Troy Downing led the measure in the House. Downing stated the law gives the tribe greater flexibility to advance long-overdue projects and strengthens Montana water infrastructure. With the bill enacted, the tribe and federal agencies will begin setting up decentralized local water projects and planning hydropower development.

The second law, S. 283, is the Illegal Red Snapper and Tuna Enforcement Act. Introduced by Senate Commerce Committee Chairman Ted Cruz along with Senators Brian Schatz, Katie Britt, Tommy Tuberville, and Roger Wicker, the bill aims to curb unauthorized foreign fishing in American waters. It incorporated language from the SUSHI Act, authored by Representative Brian Babin.

The measure directs the Department of Commerce, through the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration and the National Institute of Standards and Technology, to build a standardized chemical testing method. This testing identifies the geographic origin of imported seafood. The law requires federal scientists to package that process into a portable, one-person field test kit so state and federal maritime officers can quickly verify whether fish were taken from domestic or foreign waters. The agencies will conduct pilot studies on red snapper and tuna shipments to verify accuracy and turnaround speeds.

According to Senate Commerce Committee records, lawmakers targeted operations involving small boats called lanchas crossing into Gulf waters off Texas, where poached catches are mixed into domestic seafood supply chains. Cruz stated the law stops illicit networks from undercutting domestic businesses, while Babin noted it gives officers scientific tools to detect seafood fraud.

The third law, S. 2403, is the Retire through Ownership Act, authored by Senators Roger Marshall and Tim Kaine, with companion legislation led in the House by Representative Rick Allen. Taking effect immediately, the law amends Section 3(18) of the Employee Retirement Income Security Act of 1974, known as ERISA.

According to reports from The Fulcrum and LegiStorm, the legislation creates statutory definitions and valuation standards for adequate consideration, which is the fair market price paid for company shares. Under the new statute, fiduciaries managing Employee Stock Ownership Plans may rely in good faith on valuations of non-public stock performed by independent appraisers using the Internal Revenue Service Revenue Ruling 59-60 framework. Legal analyses from DeWitt LLP and statements from the ESOP Association noted that the Department of Labor had spent five decades without issuing a formal regulatory definition for adequate consideration, leaving founders and plan trustees exposed to audits and litigation. The new law does not eliminate trustees' duty to act prudently, nor does it remove the Department of Labor's baseline authority to issue future valuation rules.

All three measures cleared Congress in September after passing the Senate and winning voice or roll-call votes in the House, where House Speaker Mike Johnson signed them before sending them to the desk.
