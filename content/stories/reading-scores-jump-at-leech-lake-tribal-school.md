When the pop hit "Walking on Sunshine" plays over the loudspeakers at Bug-O-Nay-Ge-Shig School, elementary students do not head outside. They move into reading classrooms grouped strictly by skill rather than age or grade level.

The tribally run K-12 school serves roughly 180 students on the Leech Lake Reservation. As reported by Olivia Chilkoti and Mathew Holding Eagle III for MPR News, in coverage connected to Season 2 of the APM Reports podcast Sold a Story, the school made a sharp pivot after pandemic disruptions eroded academic performance. Superintendent Dan McKeon initiated the overhaul when the school's first post-pandemic graduating senior class tested at an average fifth-grade reading level.

On the advice of a University of Minnesota educational researcher, the school turned to Direct Instruction. The method is fast-paced and highly structured, relying on scripted phonics drills, choral responses, and steady repetition. McKeon and fellow administrators brought the curriculum to the building in August 2024, just weeks before the start of the academic year, after reviewing student data and watching demonstrations at a national conference.

Under this model, the daily routine runs like clockwork. Third-grade teacher Priscilla Smith leads mixed rooms of third, fourth, and fifth graders through brisk vocabulary drills, snapping and cueing words like "gigantic" before the children spell them in unison. For older students, instructor George Jones runs the Corrective Reading program, practicing passages about high-altitude climates and handing out immediate positive feedback.

The approach required adjustments from staff. Ingrid Mesarina-Tibbetts noted that instructors initially struggled with following rigid scripts, but they now see clear growth among students who had fallen behind. Bambi Brown credited the flexible grouping as vital to that success, pointing out that older elementary students who entered three to four grade levels behind have reached grade level. Gloria Mitchell added that repetitive mastery has helped students feel proud and confident in their work.

School assessments show notable measurable gains in decoding and fluency after two full years with the materials. According to MPR News, students following the curriculum's Reading Mastery track gained nearly four years of progress in foundational decoding and word-attack skills.

The school has a history rooted in community determination. Bug-O-Nay-Ge-Shig School was established in 1975 after 35 Ojibwe students walked out of the local public school district. School Board Chair Rose Robinson recalled launching the program in a small cabin using only the books students carried with them during that walkout.

McKeon and Mitchell emphasized that mastering basic literacy is only the first phase of their broader academic plan. School staff plan to connect these reading gains directly to cultural lessons, including Ojibwe language fluency, seasonal spear-fishing, and wild rice harvesting.
