Google affiliate Echo Zone, LLC and Xcel Energy have proposed a 15-year power supply agreement to support a planned 780-megawatt artificial intelligence data center in Pine Island, Minnesota. According to reporting from CNBC and Data Center Map, the facility would occupy a 482-acre site in the community of approximately 4,000 residents southeast of Minneapolis.

Under the proposed Electric Service Agreement, Google would finance 1,900 megawatts of new clean energy resources, according to MLQ.ai and utility industry reports. The planned portfolio consists of 1,400 megawatts of wind, 200 megawatts of solar, and 300 megawatts of multi-day storage. Form Energy is scheduled to supply iron-air batteries starting in 2028 if state approvals are finalized.

The electricity produced by these installations would not go exclusively to the Pine Island site. Reporting from MLQ.ai and utility industry updates noted that power would be dispatched through the Midcontinent Independent System Operator regional grid into Xcel's integrated transmission system. Google structured the arrangement around a Clean Energy Accelerator Charge, modeled on a tariff it used with NV Energy, and included a $50 million contribution to Xcel's Capacity*Connect program for distributed batteries.

Xcel Energy claims the arrangement will produce more than $1.1 billion in net savings for existing customers as Google shares grid infrastructure expenses, according to WCCO and CBS News. Amanda Peterson Corio, Google's global head of energy and power, told CNBC that the contract offers a pattern for large data centers to directly fund utility infrastructure while insulating local ratepayers. An Xcel spokesperson told WCCO that large users must pay their own way without raising rates or reducing reliability. Google also agreed to pay for at least 75 percent of its contracted capacity and cover up to eight years of demand charges if it leaves early.

The Minnesota Attorney General's Office has formally objected to the proposal. In regulatory filings reported by WCCO and CBS News, the office's Residential Utilities Division stated the deal could shift roughly $1 billion, and potentially billions of dollars, in net system costs onto residential and small-business customers. State attorneys warned of stranded-asset risk, the risk that ratepayers remain responsible for long-lived transmission lines and power plants if Google curtails its usage or departs after 15 years. The filing cited a 2025 Minnesota statute requiring that utility costs from exceptionally large electricity users be paid entirely by those customers, according to Patch.

The Attorney General's Office asked regulators to scrutinize or reject the plan, arguing that extensive trade-secret redactions conceal pricing and electrical demand figures, as reported by Patch and MLQ.ai. The Minnesota Public Utilities Commission is scheduled to hold a procedural meeting on October 22, 2026, according to MLQ.ai and Blandin on Broadband. Regulators will consider whether to refer the matter to a contested-case proceeding, which involves formal administrative hearings, and whether to require the release of redacted terms. A final commission decision on the agreement will follow later.
