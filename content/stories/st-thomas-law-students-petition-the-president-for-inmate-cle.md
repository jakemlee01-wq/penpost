Most law school homework ends up in a grading pile, but students at the University of St. Thomas School of Law send their assignments straight to the White House.

Working out of the school's downtown Minneapolis campus, certified law students in the Federal Commutations Clinic prepare and submit federal clemency petitions on behalf of incarcerated people across the country, WCCO-TV reported. Executive clemency gives the president the power to reduce or wipe out federal prison sentences.

Prominent attorney and law professor Mark Osler founded the clinic in 2011 and continues to direct it. Each academic year, the clinic enrolls a fresh cohort of law students and selects a new group of federal prisoners as clients.

Rather than chasing innocence claims or overturning wrongful convictions, the clinic takes the opposite route. It focuses on prisoners who admit their guilt and accept responsibility for their actions. As student attorney and J.D. candidate Khalil Yasin told WCCO-TV, the clinic represents people who are honest and acknowledge that what they did was wrong.

The students build personal relationships with their clients, sometimes traveling to meet them face to face. That includes frequent visits to the Federal Correctional Institution in Sandstone, Minnesota. Their work builds on decarceration opportunities created by the bipartisan First Step Act, which was signed into law in 2018.

Yasin explained that restorative justice requires separating past crimes from present character, noting that people genuinely change after 20 or 30 years. He said the work does not negate what clients did, but acknowledges what they have become.

Once students author the petitions and addendum narratives, the files head to the U.S. Office of the Pardon Attorney before moving to the desk of the president. That handoff begins a long pause. Clients often wait years to learn whether the president will grant a commutation to shorten their prison time.

The legal legwork has a track record. Over the clinic's first six years, 105 federal inmates were granted freedom through clemency petitions assembled by Osler, participating students, and law alumni, according to university records cited by WCCO-TV.

Yasin, who plans to enter corporate transactional law, told the station that the experience will permanently change the fabric of his life. He noted that the Constitution explicitly recognizes the mechanics of clemency, calling it both humane and right to extend mercy.
