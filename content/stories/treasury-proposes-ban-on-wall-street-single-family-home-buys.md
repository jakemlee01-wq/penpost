A neighborhood bidding war looks very different when the buyer down the block manages a multibillion-dollar portfolio. The U.S. Department of the Treasury has proposed a new rulemaking aimed at blocking large institutional investors from acquiring single-family homes across the country, as reported by the American Bankers Association.

The proposed framework implements Section 1001 of the bipartisan 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act passed by Congress, according to the Independent Community Bankers of America. It also codifies provisions of Executive Order 14376, signed by President Donald Trump on January 20, 2026, titled Stopping Wall Street from Competing with Main Street Homebuyers, the National Apartment Association reported.

Under the proposed rule, an institutional investor is defined as any for-profit entity engaged in renting, owning, managing, or investing that holds direct or indirect control over at least 350 qualifying properties, according to reporting from America’s Credit Unions and Investing.com. Dominion Financial reported that the rule defines a single-family home as a residential structure containing two or fewer units, while excluding manufactured housing, vacant land, and properties with three or more units.

Penalties for violations are steep. The Attorney General can issue civil fines reaching up to $1 million per violation, or three times the total purchase price of the property, whichever amount is higher, according to America’s Credit Unions and the Bipartisan Policy Center.

The Independent Community Bankers of America reported that federal officials designed the rule to curb corporate consolidation of residential neighborhoods and broaden homeownership opportunities. The outlet also noted that housing advocacy groups identified the regulation as a direct challenge to private equity firms that have repeatedly outbid individual buyers with all-cash offers.

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent stated that the Treasury is working to restore homeownership paths for American workers and families by expanding supply and curbing large corporate buyers while avoiding unnecessary market disruption. The American Bankers Association added that the plan was introduced alongside broader federal efforts to reduce transaction costs and add inventory for first-time buyers.

The rule will not force institutions to sell off their existing residential portfolios, restricting only new contracts and acquisitions executed after the enforcement start date, Dominion Financial and Investing.com reported.

The proposal also carves out exceptions. Easler Law and HousingWire reported that institutional buyers can still finance build-to-rent programs for new houses bought within 18 months of their certificate of occupancy, provided the homes were never owner-occupied. Easler Law noted that investors can also buy structurally deficient properties under a renovate-to-rent exception if they resolve all safety hazards and spend at least 15% of the purchase price on rehabilitation. In addition, KPMG reported that the rule protects ordinary corporate employee relocation programs run by Relocation Management Companies.

Congressional leaders offered contrasting reactions to the proposal. Senator Elizabeth Warren stated that the rules advance historic measures to lift housing supply, lower consumer expenses, and halt Wall Street bidding wars against everyday families. Representative Maxine Waters stated that the Treasury must get the details right so the rules protect consumers and give families a fair shot rather than creating new loopholes for institutional landlords.

According to the Federal Register and Justia Regulations, the public has a 30-day window to file comments on regulations.gov through November 23, 2026. The statutory purchase ban takes effect on January 7, 2027, and Goodwin and Dominion Financial reported that the restriction is scheduled to sunset after 15 years on January 7, 2042.
