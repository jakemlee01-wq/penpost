On Monday, Oct. 12, 2026, the Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences in Stockholm awarded the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences to American economists Robert E. Hall of Stanford University and Michael Woodford of Columbia University. The Associated Press and NPR reported that the prize recognizes the pair for research that laid the foundation of New Keynesian economics.

According to NobelPrize.org, the laureates were recognized "for microeconomic foundations of macroeconomic stabilisation." The award includes a diploma, a gold medal, and a cash prize. The Nobel Foundation and the Associated Press reported the cash award at 11 million Swedish kronor, while USA Today and the Stanford Report cited an award of 12 million Swedish kronor, or about $1.2 million, to be divided equally between the two economists.

Rather than relying solely on broad correlations between aggregate figures like total inflation and national output, Hall and Woodford rebuilt macroeconomic stabilization models from the ground up. NobelPrize.org and the Stanford Report noted that their work centered on microeconomic foundations, analyzing how individual households and businesses make choices about spending and saving.

The Nobel committee explained that these models showed how well-timed monetary and fiscal policies can smooth out economic peaks and troughs. NobelPrize.org and the Associated Press reported that their frameworks have helped central banks and governments steer through disruptions such as recent energy shocks and the pandemic.

Nobel committee member Timo Boppart stated that traditional Keynesian approaches of lowering taxes and increasing public spending "worked well, until it didn't," breaking down during the stagflation of the 1970s, according to the Associated Press. Boppart noted that 1970s stagflation shattered earlier assumptions that unemployment and inflation could be traded off against each other, creating the need for Hall and Woodford's frameworks. John Hassler stated that an important goal for governments and central banks is to reduce the size of these swings, limiting the risk that an economy stays trapped in high unemployment or loses control over inflation.

Both laureates earned their doctorates in economics from the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, with Hall graduating in 1967 and Woodford in 1983, according to MIT News.

Hall, 83, serves as the Robert and Carole McNeil Endowed Professor in Economics and Senior Fellow at the Hoover Institution, Emeritus, at Stanford University, the Stanford Report reported. According to the National Bureau of Economic Research, Hall was the founding director of its Economic Fluctuations and Growth program and chaired its Business Cycle Dating Committee for more than 30 years.

Woodford is the John Bates Clark Professor of Economics at Columbia University and previously served as co-editor of the NBER Macroeconomics Annual, the NBER reported.

Both economists expressed surprise at the announcement on Monday morning. The Stanford Report quoted Hall calling the prize "a wonderful surprise," adding that he spent his career trying to understand how the economy works by following interesting questions wherever they led. Reached by phone from New York during the announcement, Woodford told the committee that the honor was a tremendous surprise, according to the Associated Press.
