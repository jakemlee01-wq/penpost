On October 14, 2026, the University of Minnesota College of Food, Agricultural and Natural Resource Sciences announced a new machine-learning model developed in partnership with PepsiCo to improve corn processing.

The system is named CHIP-NCM, short for Corn Hybrid and Inbred Prediction of Nixtamalization Moisture Content. It evaluates raw corn kernels as they arrive at grain elevators, predicting the final moisture level of masa dough before any industrial cooking starts.

The effort is led by Professor Candice Hirsch and her team in the Department of Agronomy and Plant Genetics at the University of Minnesota Twin Cities. According to CFANS and the journal Theoretical and Applied Genetics, the tool builds on more than a decade of shared research between Hirsch's lab and PepsiCo teams based in St. Paul, Minnesota, and Plano, Texas.

The process centers on nixtamalization, an ancient Mesoamerican technique of cooking and steeping raw corn kernels in an alkaline lime-water solution before grinding them into masa dough. Getting moisture right during that step dictates the flavor, texture, and nutritional value of the food. It also controls machinability, which is how smoothly dough moves through industrial rollers and cutters without sticking, according to reporting by CFANS and the journal Cereal Chemistry.

To make those forecasts, CHIP-NCM analyzes near-infrared spectroscopy data, which measures how light reflects off raw grain. Built as an interactive Shiny R application, the tool recorded a Spearman rank correlation coefficient of 0.815 across diverse maize varieties, according to research reported in Cereal Chemistry and bioRxiv.

Hirsch stated that scanning a small sample allows plant operators to adjust cooking times or temperatures ahead of time. She noted that the model will theoretically reduce food waste by eliminating sacrificial test batches that factories normally run and discard to calibrate their equipment.

Reporting in Cereal Chemistry noted the software can be used across seed breeding programs, intake testing at grain elevators, and factory production floors. Beyond tortilla chips and masa, CFANS noted that corn derivatives are used in everyday goods such as toothpaste, clothing, sneakers, disposable diapers, and children's mattresses.

Hirsch's laboratory is also using the project's physical and predictive data to breed new corn hybrids designed for greater climate resilience.
